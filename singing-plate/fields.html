What you are looking at

Field lines

A magnetic field line shows which way a compass needle would point at each place along it. The bright pulses travel in that direction. Field lines never start or end: every one is a closed loop, or runs off to a great distance and comes back.

Ring. A current round a single ring makes lines that go up through the hole and back down round the outside. Far away, the Earth's field has this shape, and so does a bar magnet's.

Coil. Wind the wire round a ring-shaped former and the field is trapped inside it, running round the ring. Outside there is almost none, which is why power supplies and audio equipment use toroidal transformers. The field is stronger on the inner side of the former, in proportion to 1 ÷ distance from the axis.

Both. Add a current round the ring to the coil's field and each line becomes a helix that stays on its own ring-shaped surface, nested inside the others like the layers of an onion. A tokamak fusion reactor holds its plasma on surfaces like these.

The rotating field

One coil carrying alternating current makes a field that grows, shrinks and reverses along a single line. Two or three coils set at angles, with currents that peak one after another, add up to a field of steady strength that turns. A piece of metal inside is dragged round with it, with no brushes or sliding contacts. Nikola Tesla presented this to the American Institute of Electrical Engineers in May 1888, and the induction motors built on it still drive most of industry.

Resonance

Anything that can ring has a natural frequency. Push it at that frequency and each push adds to the last, so the motion builds until losses carry energy away as fast as the pushes supply it. Q is the number that says how far it builds: at resonance the response is Q times what the same push would produce slowly. A high Q also means a narrow peak and a long ring after the push stops. Each sand pattern on the plate is one of these peaks; the plate simulator on the Console adds many of them together.

Two tuned circuits

Put two matched resonators where their coils share some magnetic field and they stop behaving as two. Strike one and its energy passes to the other, then back, like two pendulums hung from the same string. Driven steadily, the pair has two peaks instead of one once the coupling passes 1 ÷ Q. A Tesla coil works this way: a capacitor discharges into a few turns of heavy wire, and that circuit hands its energy to a tall secondary coil tuned to the same frequency. If the two are not in tune, little crosses. Wireless chargers use the same idea at short range. Tesla's Wardenclyffe tower, begun in 1901 to send power across the globe, was never finished; coupling falls off quickly with distance.

What is not here

These are textbook models: a perfectly thin ring, an ideal coil with no gaps between turns, a resonator with one natural frequency, two circuits that match exactly. A real Tesla coil also has a spark gap and a secondary that acts partly as a quarter-wave line; neither is modelled. They show measured physics. Drawings of torus-shaped energy fields round the human body, or of free energy drawn from a torus, are not supported by measurement and are not modelled here.
