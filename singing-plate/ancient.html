      How to read this page

      Proportion and music are the same arithmetic

      Two strings whose lengths are in the ratio 2 to 1 sound an octave apart; 3 to 2, a fifth; 4 to 3, a fourth. A room 20 cubits by 10 has the same ratio between its sides as an octave has between its notes. That is why a building can be described in musical terms at all, and why Greek and later writers tied the two together.

      A ratio alone proves little

      Any two lengths make a ratio, and every ratio lies near some interval. Measure enough of a building and matches will turn up by chance. A proportion is strong evidence of design only when it comes out as whole numbers in the builders' own units, as with the King's Chamber in cubits. Each ratio here says whether it does, and how far it is from the pure interval. Within about 10 cents the ear hears it as in tune.

      Three kinds of statement

      Measured means someone measured it and published the result. Computed means it is worked out from the dimensions, here or in a published calculation, for an idealised shape. Claimed means it is widely said, and no measurement was found to support it. A claim is not shown as false; it is shown as unsupported.

      What a room does to sound

      Air in a closed room has notes of its own, set by its size: the longest is the one whose half wavelength just fits the longest side. Stone walls reflect nearly all the sound that reaches them, so these notes ring strongly. A chamber a few metres across lands in the range of a low voice whoever built it and why. That is the plainest reading of the 110 Hz found in Neolithic chambers, and it does not rule out that their builders noticed and used it.

      What is not here

      The models are schematic: flat faces, average sizes, no weathering or damage. Only the King's Chamber is a simple enough box for its notes to be computed. The Hypogeum in Malta and the galleries at Chavín de Huántar in Peru have published acoustic studies and irregular shapes, and are not modelled yet.
